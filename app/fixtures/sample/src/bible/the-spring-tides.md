# The Spring Tides and the Tide-Readers' Art

The water that surrounds Meddow follows patterns older than the city itself. The obvious tides -- the spring tides and neap tides, the seasons when the water swells and when it retreats -- have been recorded and studied by merchants and fishermen for generations. But beneath these familiar rhythms lie deeper patterns, ones that take years or decades or centuries to complete. The rocks along the Meddow coast have been shaped by water for so long that they remember things. The shale in the western approaches holds memories of bays that closed and opened, of channels carved by currents over decades, of the water's intention written in stone and salt.

The tide-readers of Meddow are not official institutions. They live in a narrow house by the Narrows, three people -- Kess, Imarys, and Corvin -- who have devoted years to understanding these deeper patterns. Kess, an elderly man who once captained deep-water vessels, brings experience of open water to their work. Imarys keeps bird records and astronomical charts, understanding that the water's patterns connect to movements in nature beyond the simple rise and fall of tides. Corvin watches the harbour walls at dawn, marking the water's colour as the light changes, building understanding through daily observation. Together, they have maintained tide tables carved into wooden boards, updated continuously as new observations accumulate season after season.

The tide-readers do not measure for profit or for official commissions. They measure to understand. They keep working maps annotated with notations in different hands across decades, with threads strung between points to show current vectors and seasonal shifts. They pay attention to signs others miss: how the currents shift, the way birds move their breeding grounds three years before human observers would notice the change, the colour of the water signalling pressure changes before storms arrive. This is the language of the water -- subtle, ancient, and completely real to those with patience enough to listen.

A true cartographer understands that the coastline is not fixed. The water moves continuously, eroding here, depositing silt there, reshaping what seems solid into something fluid. The spring tide in particular carries power that can accelerate changes already underway. When the spring tide arrives, the water takes possession of what it intends to reclaim. The greatest cartographers -- those like Maestro Kell -- learn to read these patterns not merely as observations of what is, but as predictions of what the water intends. They map not just the present but what the water's own deep memory tells them is coming.
