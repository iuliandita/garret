# Language and Style Sheet

The voice of this novel is formal and precise, though not coldly technical. It favours exact language -- depth soundings are specific numbers, coordinates are meaningful, measurements matter literally. The prose is never rushed. Descriptions of water, rocks, and coastline are given careful attention, suggesting that these materials deserve respect and contemplation. Metaphors are sparse and earned; most comparisons are direct. When the narrative describes something metaphorically, it does so with deliberation and meaning.

Names and forms of address follow a specific pattern. Characters are referred to by their full names on first introduction, then by family name or role thereafter. Maestro Kell is always "Maestro Kell" (the title is part of his identity), while his apprentice is "Ines" or "Ines Varo" depending on context. The harbour master is "Roland Vetch" or "Harbour Master Vetch" or simply "Vetch" once established. Thren begins as "Thren" and becomes "Thren Ashby" once he has taken on fuller responsibilities. The tide-readers are introduced with full names -- Kess, Imarys, Corvin -- and are sometimes referred to by these names or as "the tide-readers." Garrett Thorne is consistently "Thorne" or "Garrett Thorne." Aurel Danse is always "Aurel Danse" or "Danse," maintaining his formal Eastern City identity throughout. Technical titles are respected in the text: "Harbour Master" and "Maestro" are capitalized when used as titles.

The prose avoids colloquialism and contracted speech except in dialogue that calls for it. The narrative maintains a somewhat removed perspective, observing characters but not entering their private thoughts except where deeply necessary. Descriptions of internal experience are understated and tied to concrete physical sensation: fear is "a sensation of sickness in her stomach," uncertainty is "hands trembling," recognition is "something shifted in his expression." The text trusts readers to understand emotional content from behaviour rather than stating it directly.

The novel employs silence as a device. Characters often do not speak when much hangs in the balance. Dialogue is sparse and weighted. Long passages of physical action -- measuring, charting, preparing instruments -- are given equal narrative weight to conversation. This reflects the novel's understanding that action itself is a form of communication, that the work one does speaks as clearly as words.

Forms of address within dialogue are formal. Characters do not use casual nicknames or abbreviations. "Ines" calls Maestro Kell "Maestro," never adopting a more familiar form. "Thren" calls her "Ines," using her given name as appropriate between colleagues. Mother Venn is addressed with that formal title. The harbour master is addressed as "Harbour Master" or "Master Vetch" when direct address is required. This formality reflects both the professional nature of these relationships and the respect the narrative extends to its characters.

The prose avoids: casual interjections, contemporary slang, diminutives, excessive adjectives, emotional telling (rather than showing through action and physical detail), commentary that breaks the fourth wall or addresses the reader directly, humorous asides, and ornamental prose that exists only for beauty without serving meaning. It emphasizes: precise language, concrete detail, respect for materials and labour, attention to how water and stone interact with human effort, the weight of decisions and their consequences. The goal is a style that reads as honest, considered, and earned.
