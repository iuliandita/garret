# The Cartographers' Craft and Commission

Cartography in the three kingdoms is both art and necessity. A chart that is fractionally wrong can send a merchant vessel onto shallows and cost captains their ships, cargo, and lives. This weight of responsibility shapes how cartographers work. A true cartographer understands that the lines drawn on paper are not merely decoration or artistic flourish but navigation itself -- the promise that someone who follows these directions will reach their destination safely or will understand exactly why they cannot.

Most cartographic work in Meddow comes through official commission from the city council or from merchants who need surveys of trade routes and new channels. The city maintains an official archive of charts in the harbour office, managed by the Harbour Master and recorded by archivists like Mother Venn. Official charts are commissioned for specific purposes: marking the locations of fishing grounds, recording known hazards and rocks, establishing the routes that merchant vessels must follow to ensure safe passage. These charts are practical, useful, and conservative -- they record what is known and trusted, nothing more.

But some cartographers listen more deeply. Maestro Kell, the finest cartographer Meddow has produced in living memory, took his official commissions seriously but also pursued his own understanding of the water. He studied charts from forty years prior, comparing them to what he could measure in the present, looking for patterns in how the coastline changed over time. He recorded these observations in his personal papers, often in marginal notes and careful annotations that went far beyond what any city commission would require. His apprentices -- first Ines Varo and later others who came to study under her -- learned that true cartography required this dual commitment: serving the present work that pays and maintains a household, while also pursuing the deeper understanding that only patience and genuine listening can reveal.

A cartographer's apprenticeship is long and meticulous. The apprentice learns to hold a compass steady, to read the gradations on measuring poles, to prepare paper of exactly the right thickness and weight, to mix pigments that will not fade or run when salt spray touches them. But more importantly, the apprentice learns to see what the water is communicating through its movements and its marks upon the stones. This seeing cannot be taught directly. It requires years of working alongside someone who already possesses it, watching how they make decisions about where to measure and what to record, gradually developing the same instinct.
